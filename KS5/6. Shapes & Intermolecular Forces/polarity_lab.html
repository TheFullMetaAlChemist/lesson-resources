Using Polarity Lab

A suggested teaching sequence

  1. Bond explorer: start with H–H, then H–Cl and H–F. Switch from atoms and bonds to the shared-electron cloud. Ask which way the bonding electrons are attracted. Reveal δ labels and dipoles.
  2. Shape builder: choose a central atom and add surrounding atoms. Only compositions from the supported collection can be completed; incomplete selections are never shown as molecules. Bond orders, central lone pairs and charge are assigned automatically. Distinguish electron-region geometry from molecular shape. Rotate the tetrahedron: a flat screen is a projection, not a planar molecule.
  3. Molecular polarity: compare CO₂ with H₂O, then CCl₄ with CH₃Cl. Turn on bond dipoles and the resultant. Equal bond dipoles cancel only when their three-dimensional vector sum is zero.
  4. Use Predict first to hide the polarity answer, resultant arrow, vector sum and charge shading. Reveal after discussion. Individual bond arrows remain available as scaffolding.

What the visuals mean

Shared-electron cloud: a normalised, illustrative bonding-electron distribution. A smooth cloud is shifted towards the more electronegative atom. Brightness indicates more of this modelled shared-electron distribution. It is not a calculated orbital, electron trajectory or total electron density.

Charge-imbalance heat map: blue = relatively electron-poor (δ+); pink = relatively electron-rich (δ−); green = balanced in this model. Colour describes redistribution, not absolute electron density. A larger atom need not be more electronegative. Neither map is a quantum-chemical calculation or a calculated electrostatic potential surface.

Arrows: the crossed tail is the positive end; the arrowhead points towards the more electronegative, electron-rich end. Gold arrows show individual bonds. The teal arrow is their resultant. This uses the chemistry dipole-arrow convention.

Model boundaries

  • VSEPR uses ideal arrangements for 2–6 regions. For five regions, lone pairs occupy equatorial positions first; two lone pairs in an octahedral arrangement are opposite. The spreading animation interpolates to these rule-based arrangements; it is not a force simulation.
  • Water uses 104.5°, ammonia 107°, and nitrogen trifluoride about 102.5°. Other real presets are labelled idealised. VSEPR alone does not calculate exact bond angles. Expanded-valence examples retain ideal parent angles; real angles can differ. Hydronium uses an idealised tetrahedral parent arrangement, not a measured angle. Lone-pair lobes are symbols for central non-bonding regions, not measured orbitals.
  • For a bond, arrow magnitude is proportional to the Pauling electronegativity difference. Bond lengths are illustrative. Bond order does not multiply the arrow magnitude. Vector sums are qualitative, use arbitrary units and are not dipole moments in debye.
  • The vector model omits explicit lone-pair contributions and other quantum effects. It is intended to explain cancellation for these simple examples, not to predict dipole magnitudes for arbitrary species. For NH₃ and NF₃, for example, lone-pair effects help explain their very different measured dipoles.
  • The builder uses a curated collection of known single-centre molecules and ions, with explicit bond orders, central lone-pair counts and charges. It is not a general chemistry solver: unavailable combinations are unsupported, not necessarily impossible. Incomplete atom selections are shown only as an inventory, with no bonds, geometry or polarity assigned. Outer-atom lone pairs are omitted.
  • There is no universal electronegativity-difference boundary between covalent and ionic bonding. The continuous scale here should not be used as a rigid classifier.

Dative bonding and ions

Build NH₃ or H₂O, then explicitly add H⁺. The central lone pair supplies both electrons for a new bond, producing NH₄⁺ or H₃O⁺ and a +1 charge. The optional purple donor-to-acceptor arrow describes formation, not a permanently distinct kind of bond. The resulting bonds to H are equivalent. Molecular polarity labels, resultant arrows and charge heat maps are withheld for ions: cancellation of bond vectors does not remove an ion’s net charge. Hydronium angles here are idealised.
