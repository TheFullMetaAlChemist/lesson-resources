Why this is not a total-force meter

Every molecule in this library also experiences London dispersion forces. Polarisability matters, but electron count alone does not determine it.

The dipole calculation isolates one electrostatic term. It omits dispersion, induction, close-range charge detail and repulsion. Hydrogen bonding is not added to it as a separate numerical term: that could double-count interactions.

A substance’s boiling point depends on the whole liquid and its entropy change, not just one attractive pair.

What changes at melting and boiling?

Melting disrupts the long-range ordered lattice; liquid water still contains a changing hydrogen-bond network. Boiling separates molecules much further. The two O–H covalent bonds within each molecule remain intact.

A real liquid does not consist of isolated, permanent groups of five water molecules. The local five-molecule view is one snapshot within a larger network.

Teacher notes

What the model does — and does not do

1. Molecular structures and charge displays

All choices are real, neutral molecules. Simple molecular geometries use representative bond lengths and angles; organic structures are illustrative, geometry-optimised conformers. Atom radii are display radii, not van der Waals surfaces. The pink/blue cloud, atomic δ labels and gold bond arrows are an electronegativity-weighted teaching model, not calculated electrostatic potential or quantum electron density. Individual lone-pair lobes represent directions of electron-rich regions, not sharply bounded electron pairs.

The green crossed-tail chemical dipole arrow points from δ+ to δ−. Its magnitude uses a representative experimental gas-phase dipole; its direction follows the schematic molecular charge model. Organic conformers and condensed phases can have different dipoles. Neutral molecules remain neutral even when their charge distribution is uneven.

2. Quantitative permanent dipole component

The model treats two permanent dipoles as ideal points in vacuum, separated by r. The orientations are held fixed unless thermal reorientation is enabled. It does not resolve close-contact atomic forces.

U = C μ₁ μ₂ [n₁·n₂ − 3(n₁·r̂)(n₂·r̂)] / r³
C = 0.06022137 kJ mol⁻¹ nm³ D⁻²
Fᵣ = 3U/r ; convert kJ mol⁻¹ nm⁻¹ to pN using 1.66054.

n₁ and n₂ are unit vectors along the dipoles. Reversing both vectors to use the chemical-arrow convention does not change U. Negative U means the electrostatic configuration is lower in energy than infinite separation. Negative Fᵣ means the radial component is attractive; the model does not display tangential forces or torques. At a fixed orientation U scales as r⁻³ and its radial force as r⁻⁴. This is not the r⁻⁶ distance dependence often quoted for orientationally averaged interactions.

Thermal reorientation uses energy-biased random trial rotations at fixed centres (Metropolis sampling), with R = 0.00831446 kJ mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. Animation speed is arbitrary. Higher temperature competes with orientational order; it does not reduce a molecule’s intrinsic permanent dipole. No phase transition or liquid structure is predicted by this pair model.

Dispersion, induction, multipoles, short-range overlap and repulsion are absent from the numerical calculation. A zero dipole reading is not a zero total force. Hydrogen bonding is not numerically added to the dipole value, because an arbitrary additive decomposition would risk double counting.

3. Hydrogen bonds

The student-facing donor rule is the conventional A-level N–H, O–H or F–H rule, paired with an appropriate electron-rich acceptor. Broader IUPAC definitions cover other hydrogen bonds. The donor count here means individual donor H atoms, not the number of donor functional groups. The acceptor count is restricted to conventional N/O/F sites. The lone-pair display also shows non-acceptor lone pairs on S and halogens where appropriate. An acceptor atom may have more than one lone pair; this does not automatically specify how many equally strong bonds it will form.

The standard pair view fixes the ideal D–H···A angle at 180°, with the vertex at the donor H. Changing a molecule, donor, acceptor or donor direction restores this linear arrangement. Angles in the display are measured from the 3D atom positions, not copied from a slider. This does not change internal molecular angles: H–O–H within water remains about 104.5°. An optional “Investigate distorted geometry” panel allows deliberate bending away from the ideal; real hydrogen-bond networks can have non-linear contacts. The dashed H···acceptor line and geometry gauge are a qualitative visual rule. They favour a nearly straight D–H···A arrangement, approach towards a lone-pair region and an H···acceptor distance near 0.19 nm. These are not universal energetic cut-offs. Chemical identity, electron availability, competing interactions and steric access also matter. No hydrogen-bond energies are claimed. Molecular pairs are neutral illustrative species: chemical reactions, proton transfer, dissolution and ionisation are not simulated.

4. Water and comparisons

The ice model uses an ideal wurtzite oxygen lattice, with water molecules orientated to give at most two donated and two accepted hydrogen bonds internally. O–H bonds retain the chosen 104.5° molecular angle, so hydrogen bonds may depart slightly from straight. The liquid and vapour views are structural animations, not molecular dynamics. The network link detector is a geometric aid, not a measured hydrogen-bond population.

For ordinary ice near 0 °C, ρ ≈ 0.9167 g cm⁻³; liquid near 0 °C, ρ ≈ 0.9998 g cm⁻³. The equal-mass volume graphic uses these reference densities. Liquid water reaches its maximum density near 4 °C. Heat capacity is approximately 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ near room temperature; it varies with temperature. Neither density nor boiling point is predicted by the animation.
