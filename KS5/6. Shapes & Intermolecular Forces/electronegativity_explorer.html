What do the moving dots mean?

They are repeated illustrative samples for the two bonding electrons. They fade and reappear without orbiting. The shading represents a qualitative, time-averaged probability distribution. Neither the animation nor the cloud is a quantum-mechanical calculation.

Qualitative density model
Only the selected bond is modelled. Other bonds and outer non-bonding electrons are omitted; shell spacing, nucleus sizes, bond lengths and density shifts are schematic. A larger Δχ suggests greater bond polarity; it is not a force or an exact bond dipole in debye.

What moves?

The average bonding-electron density is displaced towards the more electronegative atom. Both electrons still belong to the shared bond.

Why δ⁻ and δ⁺?

The electron-rich end is partially negative. The electron-poor end is partially positive. These are partial charges, not full ionic charges.

What does the arrow show?

The chemistry dipole arrow points towards the δ⁻ end, with a crossed tail at δ⁺. It is not a force arrow.

Explain the result

Model attraction ∝ (Z − S) / r²

This Coulomb-style expression isolates electrostatic trends for a fixed negative charge. It is not an equation for electronegativity. The factor of two for a pair and other fixed constants are absorbed into the arbitrary scale. The shell control adds visible inner-electron layers; a deliberately illustrative lookup converts the number of layers to S. This is not a real electron configuration, and real shielding is not simply a fixed amount per electron or per shell.

Fluorine has more protons than carbon, so a larger nuclear charge. Both have two occupied shells and the same inner-shell configuration, so inner-shell shielding is similar. Fluorine is smaller and attracts bonding electrons more strongly.

Reveal a model answer

Chlorine has an additional occupied shell and more inner-electron shielding. Its bonding electrons are farther from the nucleus. These effects outweigh the increase in nuclear charge, so chlorine attracts the shared pair less strongly than fluorine.

Using the explorer

Teaching guide & model boundaries

A suggested learning route

Start with C and F. Hide the electronegativity values, compare protons, occupied shells, inner electrons and size, then make a prediction. Follow Period 2 to check whether the explanation holds across the period.

Then compare F and Cl, and F and I. The more electronegative atom now has fewer protons. Ask students to explain why increased shielding and distance outweigh the increase in nuclear charge down the group.

Move to real bond examples. Compare HF and HCl, keeping H as the common partner, and then contrast either with F₂. Use the one-factor model to isolate a cause, before attempting the questions.

What the real-element data mean

The Pauling values are dimensionless relative values, not measured forces. Proton numbers and neutral-atom shell occupancies are element data. The inner-electron count is the total number of electrons minus those in the highest occupied principal shell; it is not an effective nuclear-charge calculation.

Covalent radii come from the Royal Society of Chemistry data and are converted from ångströms to picometres (1 Å = 100 pm). They give a consistent size guide. A covalent radius is not the distance to a fixed shared pair: actual electron density is distributed in space and depends on the bond and chemical environment.

What the diagrams simplify

Each atom-comparison diagram shows one atom next to an illustrative bonding region. It is not a claim that the two selected atoms form a stable covalent molecule. Metals are included to show periodic electronegativity trends, not to suggest that every metal–non-metal pair is a simple covalent molecule.

The outer circles in the comparison view use the same covalent-radius scale. Inner-shell spacing and nucleus size are exaggerated for visibility. Inner dots are schematic electron markers, not orbiting particles. The shared pair is part of a possible bonding environment, not two extra electrons to add to the neutral-atom shell count.

Blue inner-electron shading represents electron–electron repulsion and screening, not a solid wall that blocks the nucleus. Outer electrons also affect shielding; it is only approximately constant across Periods 2 and 3, not exactly constant in every sense.

Bond-mode clouds and two flickering electron samples illustrate unequal sharing. They do not represent calculated orbitals, physical trajectories, quantitative charge populations or measured dipole moments. The density-shift replay is a teaching interpolation from a symmetric comparison picture, not a movie of bond formation. A polar bond does not by itself prove that a whole polyatomic molecule is polar.

Why use a separate one-factor model?

In real atoms, changing proton number can change the element, electron arrangement and size. The hypothetical model instead holds two parameters fixed. Its attraction index is proportional to (Z − S)/r². The shielding control now adds or removes 0–4 distinct inner shells. Each ring and its electron markers appear or disappear together; retained rings keep the same radius and opacity. The positive nuclear charge and shared-pair distance stay fixed when this control changes. The shell count is converted to the illustrative parameter S using 0 → 0, 1 → 2.5, 2 → 5, 3 → 6.5 and 4 → 8; these are teaching settings, not measured shielding constants. The markers are not a literal electron configuration. In real atoms, gaining occupied shells also changes size and usually nuclear charge; those linked effects are deliberately excluded here. No calculated model value is presented as Pauling electronegativity.

Scope of the periodic trend

The main focus is Periods 2 and 3 and the halogens F, Cl, Br and I. Electronegativity generally increases across a period and up a group towards fluorine. The complete periodic table has exceptions and more complex trends, especially among transition elements. Noble gases are omitted from this teaching comparison rather than assigned zero electronegativity.
