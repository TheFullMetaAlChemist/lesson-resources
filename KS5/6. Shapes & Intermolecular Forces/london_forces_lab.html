Teacher notes · What is calculated, what is simplified, and sources

What the images represent

The nucleus is fixed and the total electron charge stays equal and opposite to it. The cyan cloud represents negative charge; a temporary dipole is a redistribution, not an ion. In the density-change view, warm shading means a deficit of electrons relative to the symmetric reference, not protons migrating through the cloud.

The outer-cloud displacement is a coarse-grained collective coordinate. Inner charge is represented by a centred cloud, while the outer distribution distorts. The cyan charge-centre marker is calculated from both parts. Dipole magnitude depends on both the total charge and the separation of its centres; the centre of all the electrons need not shift in proportion to polarisability. Faint dots are charge-density samples with no one-dot-per-electron interpretation. Density textures, cloud sizes, displacement magnification and playback speed are visual choices, not atomic measurements. Very large graphical displacements are capped to keep clouds legible; the numerical dipoles and forces are not capped. Force-arrow lengths are visual emphasis, not a calibrated force scale. “Find clear imbalance” deliberately selects a conspicuously uneven snapshot; it is not an unbiased statistical sample.

Why the guided and mutual views are separate

The guided view shows the horizontal component of A’s dipole, isolates B’s response and uses pB = αBEA. Along the line joining the atoms, EA = 2pA/r³ in the model’s units. A negative electric field pushes electrons in the positive direction. The response is revealed gradually so it can be followed; the on-screen lag is not a calculated electronic response time. A held source with a static induced response is an induction demonstration, not a quantitative calculation of London energy.

In the mutual view, neither atom is permanently “the inducer”. Samples come from a coupled, three-dimensional quantum harmonic dipole model. Each average dipole is zero, but the dipoles have non-zero correlations. The force arrows are the ensemble-mean attractive force in that model, not a claim that every snapshot is attractive. Nuclei are held still to make the clouds easier to inspect.

Important quantum qualification

A ground-state isolated atom has a stationary, symmetric average electron density. Quantum fluctuations are not little classical balls following definite, randomly wandering paths. The animated snapshots and interpolated motion are a visual analogy for fluctuating charge configurations, not a solution of the many-electron time-dependent Schrödinger equation. This model does not include atomic excitation spectra, electron exchange, short-range repulsion, chemical bonding or molecular shape.

This noble-gas sequence links increasing electron number with more occupied shells and a more easily distorted outer distribution. Electron number is not a universal predictor of polarisability: how the electrons are bound matters. Do not generalise the sequence to arbitrary atoms, ions or molecules.

Model equations and scope of numerical readings

H = ½Σ(Pᵢ² + Xᵢ²) + c X₁X₂
pᵢ = √αᵢ Xᵢ;   ℏ = m = ω = 1
c = −2√(α₁α₂)/r³ along the axis
c = +√(α₁α₂)/r³ for each transverse axis
Var(X±) = 1 / [2√(1 ± c)]

The mutual view samples these Gaussian ground-state distributions. Smooth interpolation between independent normal samples is only a playback mechanism. At long range, the common-frequency model gives C₆ = ¾α₁α₂ and U ≈ −C₆/r⁶; force magnitude ≈ 6C₆/r⁷. Displayed mean forces are obtained from the exact model covariances. Numerical polarisability values are used as the model’s dimensionless response parameters; lengths, fields and dipoles are in consistent reduced model units, not literal physical units. Frequencies are deliberately held equal to isolate the polarisability effect. Force values and ratios are model results, not measured noble-gas forces or predictions of boiling points.

The same-field comparison uses a static external field with the atoms treated as independent tests. Both clouds use the same field and visual scale. The London-pair comparison instead uses two independent pairs at the same centre separation, sampled with matched random inputs. Real dispersion also depends on the frequency-dependent response; molecular shape and contact geometry matter for molecules.
